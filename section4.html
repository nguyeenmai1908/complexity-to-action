Step 1 · The line

The line we're fixing

Line 4 · The big message If everyone does their part, we can save the planet!

This line wants students to feel hopeful, and that matters. But “everyone does their part” makes every part sound the same. Section 2 showed that the power to make decisions is part of adaptive capacity, and Section 3 showed that climate risk isn't shared equally. “Save the planet” also makes climate change sound like a test we either pass or fail.

Go deeper: what research says about hope

A study of young people in Sweden (Ojala, 2012) found two kinds of hope. Hope that came from taking the problem seriously, and from trusting that people can act together, was linked to more climate action. Hope that came from denying the problem was not.

Go deeper: an analogy for your classroom

A doctor finds a serious illness. One doctor says, “There's nothing we can do.” Another says, “You'll be fine, don't worry about it.” A good doctor says, “This is serious. Here is what we know, here are the treatments, and here is what you can do.” The third doctor is giving informed hope.

Every bit counts

“Save the planet” sounds like all or nothing. But the IPCC reports that every increase in warming makes climate hazards worse. Switch between two levels of global warming and compare.

Not everyone, not equally

“Everyone does their part” sounds fair. But many of the actions in Section 2, like a town switching to wind power or a community rewetting a peatland, are things people do together, not one household at a time. Section 3 showed that the people facing the most risk are often already leading. A better message asks who has the power to make big changes, and who is already making them.

  • A slogan asks“Can we save the planet?”
  • A better message asks“How much harm can we prevent, and for whom?”

Try the three questions

Before you teach any climate action, ask three questions.

  1. Who benefits? Does it help the people facing the most harm, or mostly people who already have the most?
  2. Who decides? Are the people affected part of the decision?
  3. Does it break a loop? Does it deal with a cause, or only a symptom?

Try them on three actions from this module. Make your own guess first, then open each card to compare.

  • Rewetting Burns Bog

    Compare your answers
    Who benefits?
    People nearby, from cleaner water and slower flooding; wildlife; and everyone, because the carbon stays in the ground.
    Who decides?
    The governments that bought and manage the bog. A good follow-up question: whose traditional territory is it, and are those Nations part of the decisions?
    Does it break a loop?
    Yes. Wet peat keeps its carbon, which interrupts the drying loop from Section 1.

  • Fossil-powered air conditioning in every home

    Compare your answers
    Who benefits?
    Households that can afford it, during heat waves.
    Who decides?
    Each household, one at a time.
    Does it break a loop?
    No. The extra emissions add to warming, which feeds the loop instead of breaking it.

  • A city's cooling centres

    Compare your answers
    Who benefits?
    People who can't keep cool at home, often older adults, low-income households, and people without housing.
    Who decides?
    City governments. They work best when the people who use them help plan them.
    Does it break a loop?
    No. It protects people from a symptom, not the cause. That doesn't make it a bad action; people need protection now. It just can't be the whole answer.

Climate Change (one possible rewrite)

Grade 6 lesson slide

  1. What causes it?Burning fossil fuels warms the planet, and warming can set off loops that make it worse, like drying peatlands releasing even more carbon. But loops also have weak points where we can break them.
  2. What can we do?We can cut the pollution that causes warming, like switching to clean energy, and prepare for changes already here, like restoring wetlands that soak up floodwater. The best actions do both, and they work best when the people affected help plan them.
  3. Who is affected most?Many Indigenous Nations and lower-income countries face higher climate risks, largely because of colonialism and unequal access to resources. Many are also leading the response, like the Heiltsuk (Haíɫzaqv) Nation, whose clam gardens and Big House draw on knowledge passed down over generations.
  4. The big messageClimate change is serious, but what we do still matters, and every bit of warming we prevent helps. People around the world are already acting together, and we can join them.
